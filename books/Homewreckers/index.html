Homewreckers

Aaron Glantz (2019)

Central argument

The 2008 Financial Crisis transferred housing wealth from homeowners to banks and institutional investors. Government bank rescues, foreclosure practices, discounted asset sales, and unequal access to financing helped investors acquire distressed properties while millions lost their homes. Several beneficiaries later gained political influence under Donald Trump.

Mechanisms and evidence

1. Government-backed bank acquisitions

  • IndyMac collapsed in July 2008. The FDIC sold its banking operations to investors led by Steve Mnuchin in 2009, creating OneWest Bank.

  • The FDIC Loss-Sharing Agreement required the government to absorb portions of qualifying mortgage losses, reducing investors' exposure. The arrangement also covered qualifying loan modifications.

  • Sheila Bair, FDIC chair, advocated modifications to prevent unnecessary foreclosures.

2. Foreclosure practices

  • OneWest Bank and its subsidiary Financial Freedom faced criticism over aggressive foreclosures, including reverse mortgages.

  • Reverse Mortgages could trigger foreclosure following a borrower's death, departure from the home, or failure to pay taxes and insurance.

  • Robo-Signing and servicing errors allowed foreclosures to proceed using inadequately verified documents.

  • Sandy Jolley investigated OneWest's foreclosure practices after her family's experience.

3. Institutional acquisition of housing

  • Blackstone, led by Stephen Schwarzman, established Invitation Homes and purchased tens of thousands of houses after the crash.

  • Institutional investors used access to capital, cash purchases, and foreclosure auctions to acquire properties at a scale unavailable to most households.

  • Tom Barrack's Colony Capital also acquired distressed housing through corporate entities that obscured ultimate ownership.

  • Houses previously owned by families became rental properties, transferring future appreciation and rental income to investors.

4. Unequal access to financial relief

  • Banks could sell foreclosed properties at discounts substantially below former owners' mortgage balances, while homeowners often struggled to obtain comparable principal reductions.

  • Government programs including TARP stabilized financial institutions; HAMP offered mortgage assistance but depended heavily on lenders and servicers.

  • Glantz questions why public intervention more effectively protected financial assets than existing homeownership.

5. Financialization of rental housing

  • Invitation Homes helped develop Single-Family Rental Securitization, using rental portfolios and their expected income to support securities sold to investors.

  • Wall Street could finance and profit from rent payments rather than solely from homeowners' mortgage payments.

  • Large landlords gained access to additional capital for acquiring properties.

6. Racial disparities

  • Redlining and discriminatory mortgage lending restricted Black and Latino households' access to affordable credit and homeownership.

  • Predatory and high-cost lending increased exposure to foreclosure.

  • The loss of home equity compounded existing racial wealth disparities.

7. Political influence

  • Steve Mnuchin became Treasury Secretary; Wilbur Ross became Commerce Secretary; Stephen Schwarzman chaired Trump's Strategic and Policy Forum; Tom Barrack chaired his inaugural committee.

  • Fred Trump had previously built substantial real-estate wealth with government housing assistance.

  • Glantz connects government-supported property accumulation with the political influence of wealthy investors.

Historical alternative

  • The Home Owners' Loan Corporation (1933) purchased and refinanced distressed mortgages to prevent foreclosures.

  • Glantz contrasts this direct intervention with the post-2008 emphasis on financial stabilization and investor acquisitions.

  • New Deal housing policy also reinforced racial discrimination.

Arguments challenged

  • Irresponsible borrowers caused the crisis: Risky lending, servicing practices, and financial incentives also contributed.

  • Foreclosure was unavoidable: Mortgage modifications and principal reductions offered alternatives in some cases.

  • Bank rescues benefited everyone equally: Financial institutions received protections that did not necessarily preserve household ownership.

  • Corporate landlords simply restored housing markets: Their purchases also concentrated ownership and redirected appreciation and rental income.

  • Real-estate fortunes were purely self-made: Public guarantees, subsidies, and financial rescues helped create opportunities for private accumulation.

Further questions

  • Did the FDIC's loss-sharing terms make foreclosure financially preferable to modification in particular cases?

  • Why could investors purchase distressed properties at discounts unavailable to existing homeowners?

  • How much institutional housing consolidation resulted from government policy versus market conditions?

  • Which specific policies resulted from the political influence of the investors Glantz profiles?
