Homewreckers

Notes

This rivals The Israel Lobby and U.S. Foreign Policy for the book I am most frustrated about not being able to recall most of. For that reason, I have gone back through a summary of this one to take my notes again.

The book investigates how Wall Street investors, banks, private equity firms, and government officials profited from the 2008 Financial Crisis and the subsequent foreclosure epidemic.

Its central argument is that the crisis was not simply the result of irresponsible borrowers or an unavoidable market collapse. Government policies and financial institutions helped create the conditions for mass foreclosure, then enabled wealthy investors to acquire distressed homes at enormous discounts.

A particularly important thread is the emergence of large corporate landlords and the connection between the foreclosure crisis and the political rise of Donald Trump.

Key organizations and vocabulary

Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS) — Financial products created by bundling mortgages and selling claims on their payments to investors.

Collateralized Debt Obligations (CDOs) — Structured financial products that can repackage mortgage-related debts into different risk categories.

Underwater Mortgage — A mortgage whose outstanding balance exceeds the home's market value.

TARP — Troubled Asset Relief Program, the 2008 federal financial rescue program.

HAMP — Home Affordable Modification Program, designed to help struggling homeowners obtain mortgage modifications.

Private Equity — Investment firms that acquire and manage assets using pooled capital.

Single-Family Rental (SFR) — Houses owned and rented out, increasingly as a large-scale institutional investment.

People

Steve Mnuchin - Former Goldman Sachs executive. - Led an investor group that purchased failed mortgage lender IndyMac from the government in 2009. - Renamed the institution OneWest Bank. - OneWest became notorious for aggressive foreclosure practices. - The government agreed to share certain losses on the bank's troubled loans. - Became Donald Trump's Treasury Secretary in 2017.

Stephen Schwarzman - Cofounder and CEO of Blackstone. - Blackstone became one of America's largest institutional owners of single-family rental houses. - Created Invitation Homes. - Between 2012 and 2014, Blackstone purchased tens of thousands of houses, many previously foreclosed. - Close adviser to Trump during his first presidency.

Tom Barrack - Founder of Colony Capital. - Longtime friend and adviser of Donald Trump. - His investment firms acquired distressed real estate and single-family rental properties.

Jamie Dimon - CEO of JPMorgan Chase. - JPMorgan acquired Washington Mutual's banking operations after its failure.

Sheila Bair - Chair of the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) from 2006 to 2011. - Advocated mortgage modifications to prevent unnecessary foreclosures. - Oversaw the FDIC during the collapse and sale of IndyMac. - Important for understanding the tension between helping homeowners and protecting the financial system.

Donald Trump - Real estate developer who publicly anticipated opportunities to purchase distressed property during a housing crash. - His father Fred Trump built a real estate business with significant government assistance. - Donald's political rise connected several of the investors discussed in the book. - Appointed Mnuchin and Ross to his cabinet.

Part I: Financial Freedom

The Salesman

  • Reverse Mortgages — Loans allowing older homeowners to borrow against their home equity, generally without monthly mortgage payments.

  • Financial Freedom — Major reverse mortgage lender that became part of the broader foreclosure story.

  • Home Equity — Difference between a property's value and the debt secured against it.

  • Reverse mortgages were promoted as a way for older Americans to remain in their homes while accessing accumulated wealth.

  • These products could also expose vulnerable homeowners to foreclosure if they failed to satisfy loan conditions, such as paying property taxes or insurance.

  • Establishes the book's recurring contrast between financial products marketed as helping people and the incentives of the institutions selling them.

A Squandered Opportunity

  • Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) — New Deal agency established in 1933.

  • HOLC purchased or refinanced troubled mortgages, helping many homeowners avoid foreclosure.

Government intervention could be used to preserve homeownership rather than simply stabilize banks.

Historical complication: New Deal housing institutions also participated in or reinforced racially discriminatory housing practices.

Glantz contrasts this intervention with the government's response after 2008.

A Run on the Bank

IndyMac Bank — Major mortgage lender that failed in July 2008.

FDIC — Federal agency that insures bank deposits and resolves failed banks.

Subprime Mortgage Crisis — Deterioration of risky mortgage loans that contributed to widespread financial instability.

IndyMac's collapse became one of the largest bank failures in American history.

The FDIC took control of the institution.

IndyMac's mortgage portfolio included loans that became central to later foreclosure controversies.

Life on Park Avenue

Steve Mnuchin — Former Goldman Sachs banker.

Goldman Sachs — Investment bank with extensive ties to Wall Street and government.

Eddie Lampert — Hedge fund manager associated with Sears and Kmart; Mnuchin previously worked with him.

Examines the wealthy financial circles from which investors such as Mnuchin emerged.

Their access to capital, relationships, and financial expertise positioned them to purchase distressed assets unavailable to ordinary households.

The Vultures Circle

J. Christopher Flowers — Private equity investor specializing in financial institutions.

Wilbur Ross — Distressed-asset investor.

Steve Mnuchin — Led the IndyMac acquisition group.

IndyMac Acquisition (2009)

Investor consortium purchased IndyMac's banking operations.

Bank subsequently became OneWest Bank.

Loss-Sharing Agreement — FDIC arrangement in which the government agrees to absorb a specified portion of losses on covered assets.

Glantz argues that the structure of the transaction offered investors extraordinary opportunities for profit while leaving taxpayers exposed to risk.

Important nuance: loss sharing did not mean the government paid investors for every foreclosure; it covered qualifying losses under contractual terms.

Part II: Foreclosure Kings

A Mortgage Discovered

Financial Freedom — Reverse mortgage company eventually owned by OneWest.

Reverse Mortgage Foreclosure — Can occur after a borrower dies, moves out, or fails to meet certain obligations.

The chapter begins uncovering how mortgage paperwork and servicing decisions could determine whether families lost their homes.

Important distinction between the company originating a mortgage and the company later servicing or owning it.

Foreclosure Machine

OneWest Bank — Institution created from the acquisition of IndyMac.

Steve Mnuchin — Chairman of OneWest.

Robo-Signing — Mass production or signing of foreclosure documents without adequate verification.

Mortgage Servicing — Collecting payments, managing accounts, and handling defaults on behalf of mortgage owners.

Loan Modification — Changing mortgage terms to help a borrower continue making payments.

Foreclosure procedures became highly automated and bureaucratic.

Errors in documentation and servicing could have catastrophic consequences for homeowners.

Glantz argues that foreclosure could be more profitable or administratively convenient for institutions than helping families remain in their homes.

Sandy Jolley's investigations provide an individual perspective on these systemic practices.

Part III: The Greatest Thing I've Ever Done

ColFin AI-CA5 LLC

Shell Companies — Legal entities used to own assets, often making the ultimate owners difficult to identify.

Colony Capital — Investment firm founded by Tom Barrack.

Tom Barrack — Central figure in distressed real estate investment.

Investors purchased foreclosed houses through complicated corporate structures.

The names on property records often concealed the recognizable investors behind the transactions.

Homes increasingly became entries in enormous institutional portfolios rather than individually owned residences.

Polo Ponies and Wine

Tom Barrack — Wealthy investor with connections to prominent political and business figures.

Donald Trump — Longtime acquaintance of Barrack.

The chapter explores the social world of wealthy real estate investors.

Connections between financiers, politicians, and elite social circles helped create access to deals and capital.

Contrast between investors' luxurious lifestyles and the economic circumstances of people losing their homes.

For Infinity

Blackstone — Major private equity firm.

Stephen Schwarzman — Blackstone cofounder.

Invitation Homes — Blackstone-backed single-family rental company.

Institutional Landlords — Companies owning large portfolios of rental properties.

Following the crash, investment firms purchased large quantities of foreclosed homes.

Houses were converted into rental properties rather than resold to prospective owner-occupants.

This created a new model of large-scale corporate ownership in the American housing market.

Son of a Linen Store Owner

Stephen Schwarzman

Grew up in Pennsylvania.

Cofounded Blackstone with Peter G. Peterson in 1985.

Became one of the most influential figures in private equity.

Blackstone — Expanded into real estate and distressed assets.

The chapter places Schwarzman's rise in the broader history of private equity.

Blackstone's enormous access to capital allowed it to buy properties at a scale that individual homebuyers could not match.

Loading the Boat

Invitation Homes — Single-family rental platform built by Blackstone.

Foreclosure Auctions — Properties sold following foreclosure, often to cash buyers.

Cash Buyers — Investors able to purchase without mortgage financing.

Large firms accumulated houses rapidly in markets hit hardest by the crash.

Investors could purchase properties in bulk and operate them as rental portfolios.

The foreclosure crisis became an opportunity to consolidate ownership of residential real estate.

Life on a Lease

Corporate Landlords — Large firms managing rental housing portfolios.

Invitation Homes — Major example.

Families who once owned homes increasingly rented from investors.

Tenants could face fees, maintenance problems, and difficulties dealing with distant property management companies.

Glantz argues that the change from owner-occupancy to corporate renting undermined opportunities to build intergenerational wealth.

The distinction between paying a mortgage and paying rent becomes central:

Mortgage payments can build equity.

Rent generally does not build ownership in the property.

Part IV: The New Redlining

A Bungalow in Los Angeles

Redlining — Historic practice of denying or restricting financial services in neighborhoods based on race or other characteristics.

Federal Housing Administration (FHA) — Federal agency that insures qualifying mortgages.

Home Owners' Loan Corporation — Associated with historical neighborhood risk maps later linked to discriminatory lending.

Racial Wealth Gap — Persistent differences in accumulated wealth between racial groups.

Black and Latino communities experienced disproportionate harm from predatory lending and foreclosure.

Los Angeles — Important setting for examining unequal access to homeownership.

The book connects historic discrimination in mortgage lending to modern disparities in homeownership and credit access.

Loss of home equity disproportionately harmed families whose homes represented their primary source of wealth.

Part V: The Big Flip

Time to Make a Deal

Distressed Assets — Properties or debts sold at discounts because of financial difficulty.

Private Equity — Investment firms using large pools of capital to acquire assets.

Investors could acquire houses for far less than the outstanding balances of their former owners' mortgages.

The government often helped stabilize financial institutions rather than arranging equivalent discounts for struggling homeowners.

Glantz questions why distressed homes could be sold cheaply to investors when existing homeowners were frequently denied comparable opportunities to restructure their debt.

The Community Fights Back

Foreclosure Resistance — Activism aimed at preventing evictions and challenging lending abuses.

Community Reinvestment Act (1977) — Federal law encouraging regulated banks to meet the credit needs of communities they serve, consistent with safe and sound banking.

Community groups organized against foreclosure, predatory lending, and the consolidation of housing by investors.

Homeowners, activists, and attorneys challenged banks through protests, legal action, and public pressure.

Some communities sought ways to preserve affordable housing and neighborhood ownership.

The New Debt Bundles

Securitization — Packaging financial assets into securities sold to investors.

Mortgage-Backed Securities — Mortgage loans bundled into tradable investments.

Single-Family Rental Securitization — Securities backed by rental income from portfolios of houses.

The financial industry developed new securities tied to corporate-owned rental housing.

Rather than simply lending to homeowners, Wall Street could now profit from rent paid by households that had lost access to ownership.

The new financial structures raised concerns about reproducing risks associated with the pre-2008 mortgage market.

Too Big to Fail

JPMorgan Chase — Major financial institution led by Jamie Dimon.

Jamie Dimon — Chairman and CEO of JPMorgan Chase.

Washington Mutual (WaMu) — Large savings and loan institution that failed in 2008.

Too Big to Fail — Concern that certain financial institutions cannot be allowed to collapse without causing wider economic damage.

Bank Bailouts — Government assistance intended to stabilize the financial system.

Major banks emerged from the crisis with significant economic and political power.

Glantz argues that legal and regulatory responses did not adequately hold financial executives accountable for widespread mortgage abuses.

The chapter emphasizes the difference between protecting financial stability and protecting individual homeowners.

Part VI: Enter the Donald

An Early Vulture

Fred Trump — Real estate developer and father of Donald Trump.

Federal Housing Administration — Government-backed mortgage insurance helped support the development of residential housing.

Donald Trump — Entered real estate through his father's business.

Government Subsidies — Public support for private housing development through loans, guarantees, tax benefits, and other arrangements.

The Trump family's real estate wealth was closely connected to government housing policy.

Glantz challenges the narrative of purely self-made real estate fortunes.

He connects earlier forms of government-supported real estate accumulation to the opportunities investors received after 2008.

Trump University

Trump University — For-profit real estate education business established by Donald Trump.

Marketed expensive seminars promising to teach people real estate investing techniques.

Faced lawsuits alleging deceptive and fraudulent business practices.

Trump University Settlement (2016) — Trump agreed to a $25 million settlement without admitting wrongdoing.

The business illustrates the marketing of real estate speculation as a route to wealth.

Glantz contrasts promises of accessible prosperity with the reality of a housing market increasingly dominated by wealthy investors.

Part VII: President Trump

Triumph of the Homewreckers

Donald Trump — Elected president in 2016.

Steve Mnuchin — Appointed Secretary of the Treasury.

Wilbur Ross — Appointed Secretary of Commerce.

Stephen Schwarzman — Chaired Trump's Strategic and Policy Forum.

Tom Barrack — Chaired Trump's inaugural committee.

Jared Kushner — Real estate investor and Trump's son-in-law; became a senior White House adviser.

Sean Hannity — Conservative media personality with significant real estate investments.

Revolving Door — Movement of individuals between private-sector leadership and influential government positions.

The investors who benefited from the housing crisis gained direct access to the highest levels of government.

Glantz presents this as the culmination of a long process in which wealth generated political influence, which could in turn protect opportunities for further wealth accumulation.

Epilogue: A Path to Shared Prosperity

Glantz argues that government policy could prioritize widespread homeownership rather than the accumulation of property by institutional investors.

HOLC — Historical example of direct intervention to prevent foreclosure.

Affordable Housing — Housing that households can reasonably afford relative to income.

Community Land Trusts — Nonprofit or community-controlled arrangements intended to preserve long-term housing affordability.

Mortgage Modification — Restructuring loan terms to prevent unnecessary foreclosure.

Housing Cooperatives — Shared ownership arrangements that can offer alternatives to traditional private ownership and renting.

Central question: Why did the government help investors purchase distressed houses rather than helping more of the original occupants retain them?

Events and policies worth remembering

Further Questions

Why did the federal government not use a program resembling the Home Owners' Loan Corporation after the 2008 crisis?

What exactly were the terms of the FDIC's loss-sharing agreement with OneWest Bank? How much did the government ultimately pay?

Were banks legally obligated to modify mortgages, or could they choose foreclosure even when modification would recover more money?

How did Blackstone and Invitation Homes change the housing market in cities like Phoenix, Atlanta, and Las Vegas?

What is the relationship between Redlining, Subprime Lending, and the modern racial wealth gap?

How did JPMorgan Chase acquire Washington Mutual, and what liabilities did it assume?

Why did so few senior financial executives face criminal prosecution after the 2008 crisis?

How much of the rise in corporate landlords was driven by federal policy versus falling property prices and private investment?

How has institutional ownership of single-family houses changed since the book was published in 2019?

Connections to The Jakarta Method and The Israel Lobby

The Jakarta Method explores how American power supported anticommunist governments and economic arrangements abroad. Homewreckers explores how American public institutions and financial policy helped determine which private actors benefited from an economic crisis at home.

Both books invite questions about the relationship between government action and private wealth, particularly when policies presented as stabilizing or protecting society disproportionately benefit powerful groups.

The Israel Lobby provides another angle: how political influence shapes public policy. The three books examine different mechanisms of power—covert intervention, organized lobbying, and financial influence—without necessarily establishing that these mechanisms operate identically.

A useful recurring question across all three: Who has access to government decision-makers, who benefits from the resulting policies, and who bears their costs?
