The Shock Doctrine

Naomi Klein (2007)

Central argument

Naomi Klein argues that governments and corporations exploit wars, coups, economic crises, and natural disasters to impose unpopular economic reforms. She calls this Disaster Capitalism: crises weaken opposition, enabling privatization, deregulation, austerity, and transfers of public wealth to private investors.

The book traces this pattern from Augusto Pinochet's Chile to post-Soviet Russia, the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis, the Iraq War, and Hurricane Katrina.

Mechanisms and evidence

1. Chicago School economics and dictatorship

  • Milton Friedman and the Chicago School of Economics advocated privatization, deregulation, and reduced government intervention.

  • Following the 1973 Chilean Coup, Augusto Pinochet established a dictatorship and appointed Chicago-trained economists, the Chicago Boys, to implement market reforms.

  • The regime suppressed unions and political opposition. Operation Condor coordinated repression across South American dictatorships.

  • Klein argues that state violence weakened resistance to economic restructuring.

2. Thatcherism and the Falklands War

  • Margaret Thatcher faced substantial opposition to privatization and restrictions on trade unions.

  • Victory in the Falklands War (1982) strengthened her popularity and political authority.

  • Her government subsequently accelerated privatization and defeated the British Miners' Strike (1984–1985).

  • Klein argues that military victory facilitated previously unpopular domestic reforms.

3. Bolivia and shock therapy

  • Bolivia's hyperinflation prompted President Víctor Paz Estenssoro to introduce Decree 21060 in 1985.

  • Advised by Jeffrey Sachs, the government liberalized prices, restricted wages, and reduced public spending.

  • Inflation fell sharply, but restructuring caused widespread hardship, particularly among miners.

  • Klein argues that emergency stabilization enabled broader market reforms.

4. Poland, China, and South Africa

  • After Poland's democratic transition, Leszek Balcerowicz introduced rapid price liberalization and privatization, departing from the worker-centered economic vision of parts of Solidarity.

  • In China, Deng Xiaoping pursued market reforms while maintaining authoritarian rule. The government suppressed the Tiananmen Square Protests in 1989.

  • In South Africa, the African National Congress achieved majority-rule democracy in 1994, but existing debt, property rights, and financial arrangements constrained economic redistribution.

  • Klein argues that market liberalization does not necessarily produce democracy or economic equality.

5. Russian privatization

  • Following the Soviet Union's collapse, Boris Yeltsin introduced rapid price liberalization and privatization amid severe economic hardship.

  • During the 1993 Russian Constitutional Crisis, Yeltsin dissolved parliament and used military force against parliamentary opponents.

  • The 1995 Loans-for-Shares program transferred stakes in valuable state enterprises to politically connected banks.

  • Klein argues that privatization concentrated public wealth among Russian Oligarchs while weakening democratic oversight.

6. Asian financial crisis and IMF intervention

  • The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis spread from Thailand to Indonesia, South Korea, and other economies.

  • The International Monetary Fund attached austerity, financial restructuring, and market liberalization conditions to emergency loans.

  • Foreign investors acquired distressed businesses at reduced prices.

  • Klein argues that financial crises gave international lenders and investors leverage over domestic economic policy and ownership.

7. September 11 and privatized security

  • Following the September 11 Attacks, the United States expanded military, intelligence, and counterterrorism spending.

  • The Department of Homeland Security was established in 2002, creating extensive demand for private security and surveillance contractors.

  • Companies including Halliburton and Blackwater benefited from expanded government contracting.

  • Klein argues that privatization created businesses financially dependent on continued security spending.

8. Iraq's occupation and reconstruction

  • Following the 2003 Invasion of Iraq, the Coalition Provisional Authority, led by Paul Bremer, reorganized Iraq's economy.

  • CPA Order 39 permitted extensive foreign ownership and profit repatriation; CPA Order 12 suspended tariffs and trade restrictions.

  • Halliburton and Bechtel received major reconstruction contracts amid widespread insecurity and infrastructure failures.

  • Klein argues that the occupation imposed market reforms without Iraqi democratic consent while directing reconstruction spending toward foreign contractors.

9. Natural disasters and redevelopment

  • After the 2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami, coastal restrictions in Sri Lanka prevented some fishing communities from rebuilding while tourism development expanded.

  • Following Hurricane Katrina (2005), New Orleans reorganized public education around charter schools and demolished major public housing developments.

  • Milton Friedman advocated using Katrina to introduce school-choice reforms.

  • Klein argues that displacement weakened residents' ability to oppose changes to property rights and public institutions.

10. Israel and the security economy

  • Klein describes Israel's growing security and technology industries alongside declining dependence on Palestinian labor.

  • The Israeli West Bank Barrier and restrictions on Palestinian movement expanded security infrastructure while limiting Palestinian economic activity.

  • She argues that profitable surveillance and military industries can reduce some economic incentives for ending prolonged conflict.

Historical alternative

  • During the Great Depression, Franklin D. Roosevelt introduced the New Deal, expanding public employment, regulation, and social protections.

  • Klein contrasts this with crisis responses centered on privatization and austerity.

Arguments challenged

  • Market reforms require democratic consent: Chile, Russia, and Iraq demonstrate how reforms can proceed through authoritarian or externally imposed authority.

  • Economic liberalization produces democracy: China expanded markets without political democratization.

  • IMF rescue packages are politically neutral: Loan conditions can impose lasting changes to domestic economic policy.

  • Privatization distributes economic power: Russia's transition concentrated public assets among politically connected investors.

  • Private contractors improve government efficiency: Iraq's reconstruction revealed failures in accountability and service delivery.

  • Disasters justify rebuilding institutions without residents' consent: Displaced communities retain claims to housing, property, and public services.

Further questions

  • Which Chilean economic reforms depended on authoritarian repression?

  • Did IMF conditions improve or worsen recovery during the Asian financial crisis?

  • Could Russia have privatized state enterprises without concentrating ownership among oligarchs?

  • How did foreign contractors compare with Iraqi institutions in reconstruction outcomes?

  • How did post-Katrina reforms affect displaced New Orleans residents?
