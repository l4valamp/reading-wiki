White Trash

Nancy Isenberg (2016)

Central argument

American class hierarchy predates independence. British colonization relied on transporting the poor as disposable labor, while colonial landownership, inheritance, and political institutions concentrated wealth. The American Revolution replaced hereditary aristocracy with republican ideals but did not eliminate inherited economic inequality.

Isenberg traces how elites repeatedly portrayed poor whites as lazy, uncivilized, or biologically inferior to justify their exclusion. Frontier expansion, slavery, eugenics, New Deal reform, and modern political culture changed the language of class without eliminating its structural foundations. The myth of universal upward mobility obscured the importance of inherited property and political power.

Mechanisms and evidence

1. Colonization and disposable labor

  • English promoters including Richard Hakluyt advocated sending vagrants, convicts, and the unemployed to America. Colonies would remove supposedly unproductive people from England while supplying cheap labor and imperial wealth.

  • In Jamestown, indentured servants exchanged years of labor for passage. High mortality, harsh discipline, and manipulated contracts undermined promises of eventual independence.

  • The Headright System awarded Virginia land for importing laborers, allowing wealthy sponsors to accumulate acreage while servants remained dependent.

2. Landownership and colonial hierarchy

  • John Locke helped draft the 1669 Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina, which proposed hereditary ranks of landgraves and caciques, large estates, and hereditary servitude.

  • William Byrd II portrayed North Carolina's landless squatters as idle and uncivilized. His 1728 boundary survey exposed the contempt of Virginia's landed elite toward poorer settlers.

  • James Oglethorpe founded Georgia as a colony intended to rehabilitate Britain's poor through agricultural labor. Restrictions on slavery and large landholdings faced planter opposition; Georgia legalized slavery in 1751.

3. Republican equality and inherited class

  • Benjamin Franklin promoted industriousness, marriage, and population growth as foundations of colonial prosperity, while treating idleness and dependency as social threats.

  • Thomas Jefferson celebrated independent farmers but proposed selecting talented children from poorer families for public education, leaving most of the class hierarchy intact.

  • Jefferson's Notes on the State of Virginia linked cultivation, environment, and human improvement. His ideas about breeding and racial inheritance contradicted his egalitarian political language.

4. Frontier expansion and Jacksonian democracy

  • Western squatters occupied Indigenous territory before formal land sales. Speculators and wealthy purchasers frequently acquired the best land, displacing poorer settlers.

  • Andrew Jackson cultivated an image as a frontier common man despite his plantation wealth and slaveholding. His political rise helped transform the backwoodsman into a democratic symbol.

  • The Indian Removal Act of 1830 facilitated white settlement and land speculation while forcibly dispossessing Native nations. Expansion did not guarantee equal access to property.

5. Slavery and the poor white class

  • Southern elites used labels such as crackers, clay-eaters, and poor white trash to distinguish landless whites from respectable slaveholding families.

  • Hinton Rowan Helper argued in The Impending Crisis of the South (1857) that slavery impoverished nonslaveholding whites by concentrating land, labor, and political influence among planters.

  • James Henry Hammond defended slavery through his 1858 Mudsill Theory, claiming every society required a permanent laboring class.

  • During the American Civil War, Confederate conscription exemptions for men supervising twenty or more enslaved people intensified resentment over wealthy slaveholders' privileges.

6. Eugenics and hereditary poverty

  • After the Civil War, elite commentators increasingly described poor whites through inherited degeneracy, defective bloodlines, and supposedly inferior reproduction.

  • Francis Galton's eugenic theories influenced American reformers who treated poverty, disability, and criminality as hereditary problems.

  • In Buck v. Bell (1927), the Supreme Court upheld Virginia's compulsory sterilization law. Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. endorsed preventing people classified as genetically unfit from reproducing.

  • Carrie Buck, a poor Virginia woman, became the test case for state control over reproduction, demonstrating how class prejudice acquired medical and legal authority.

7. The Great Depression and government reform

  • The Great Depression exposed widespread tenant farming, landlessness, and rural poverty, challenging claims that hard work guaranteed independence.

  • Rexford Tugwell and the Resettlement Administration pursued planned communities and agricultural resettlement to address structural poverty rather than individual moral failure.

  • Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal challenged older assumptions about government responsibility, but agricultural reform faced resistance from landlords and entrenched local elites.

  • Sociologist Howard W. Odum investigated the category of poor whites, documenting how regional stereotypes combined economic circumstances with judgments about character and ancestry.

8. Postwar prosperity and the country-boy image

  • Postwar homeownership and consumer prosperity reinforced the ideal of a universal middle class, even as trailer residents faced exclusion through zoning and local housing restrictions.

  • Elvis Presley transformed working-class southern identity into commercially successful popular culture. Rural origins became marketable without eliminating class stigma.

  • Orval Faubus and George Wallace used rural populist identities and resistance to desegregation to mobilize white voters.

  • Lyndon B. Johnson drew on his impoverished Texas Hill Country background to promote the War on Poverty, while confronting opposition to federal intervention.

9. Redneck identity as entertainment and politics

  • Deliverance (1972) portrayed Appalachian backwoods residents through violence and degeneracy, reviving older stereotypes of isolated rural populations.

  • Jimmy Carter's presidential image emphasized rural authenticity, while his brother Billy Carter became a national caricature of the southern redneck.

  • Dolly Parton, NASCAR, and White Trash Cooking (1986) helped turn stigmatized working-class imagery into entertainment and consumer identity.

  • Bill Clinton's Arkansas upbringing became a recurring explanation for his ambition and sexual behavior. Comparisons with Thomas Jefferson revealed persistent assumptions about breeding and respectability.

  • Sarah Palin's 2008 vice-presidential campaign revived the country-outsider identity, presenting rural authenticity as a political qualification while attracting class-based ridicule.
