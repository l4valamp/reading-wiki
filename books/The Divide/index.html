The Divide

Matt Taibbi (2014)

Central argument

American criminal justice operates under two increasingly unequal systems. Poor people, particularly racial minorities and immigrants, face aggressive policing, surveillance, and punishment for minor offenses, while wealthy individuals and corporations frequently resolve serious financial misconduct through civil settlements without criminal prosecution.

Taibbi argues that this divide reflects institutional choices rather than simply differences in criminal behavior. Financial regulators increasingly negotiate with powerful institutions, while police, immigration authorities, and welfare agencies expand enforcement against vulnerable populations. The result is a system in which poverty invites scrutiny and wealth provides protection from it.

Mechanisms and evidence

1. Selective prosecution after the financial crisis

  • In 2012, Manhattan District Attorney Cyrus Vance Jr. charged Abacus Federal Savings Bank, a small Chinese American bank, and several employees with mortgage fraud. The bank had reported suspected employee misconduct and its mortgages had continued performing.

  • Major institutions including Bank of America, Citigroup, and JPMorgan Chase faced multibillion-dollar settlements over defective mortgages without comparable corporate criminal trials.

  • Taibbi contrasts the prosecution of Abacus with regulators' reluctance to prosecute systemically important financial institutions, whose collapse was considered a potential economic threat.

2. HSBC money laundering and marijuana arrests

  • In 2012, HSBC agreed to pay approximately $1.9 billion after investigations uncovered failures to prevent money laundering involving Mexican drug cartels and transactions connected to sanctioned countries.

  • The Department of Justice, under Criminal Division chief Lanny Breuer, negotiated a deferred prosecution agreement rather than pursuing a corporate conviction, citing possible consequences for the financial system.

  • In New York, Tory Marone, a homeless man, was arrested after police found marijuana during a search. Although New York had decriminalized possession of small amounts, displaying marijuana in public remained punishable, creating opportunities for arrests following police searches.

  • Taibbi contrasts the treatment of minor drug possession with the settlement offered to a bank that had facilitated enormous illicit financial flows.

3. Broken-windows policing and criminal records

  • Andrew Brown, a Brooklyn resident with a criminal history, repeatedly encountered aggressive police scrutiny despite efforts to rebuild his life.

  • New York's Broken Windows Policing strategy prioritized minor offenses and public-order violations, disproportionately exposing residents of heavily policed neighborhoods to searches and arrests.

  • Minor arrests could generate court appearances, legal expenses, lost employment, and criminal records even when charges were dismissed.

  • Taibbi contrasts this enforcement with corporate misconduct cases, where negotiated settlements often avoided comparable individual consequences.

4. Lehman Brothers and financial opacity

  • Before its 2008 collapse, Lehman Brothers used Repo 105 transactions to temporarily remove tens of billions of dollars in assets from its balance sheet, making its leverage appear lower during reporting periods.

  • After Lehman's bankruptcy, Barclays acquired substantial parts of its American operations through an emergency sale whose valuation and disclosure practices generated litigation.

  • Complex transactions, private negotiations, and limited transparency made financial misconduct difficult to investigate and prosecute.

  • Taibbi argues that financial complexity effectively shields powerful institutions from scrutiny routinely imposed on ordinary defendants.

5. Immigration enforcement as routine policing

  • Section 287(g) of the Immigration and Nationality Act allowed participating local law enforcement agencies to perform federal immigration enforcement functions.

  • In Gainesville, Georgia, undocumented workers faced detention and potential deportation following routine police encounters, including traffic stops.

  • Georgia immigration restrictions and driver's-license requirements made ordinary transportation dangerous for undocumented residents, encouraging reliance on Latino-owned taxi services.

  • Immigration proceedings operated through an administrative court system with fewer procedural protections than ordinary criminal courts. Even minor encounters could lead to detention and family separation.

6. Hedge funds and the Fairfax campaign

  • Hedge funds betting against Fairfax Financial Holdings sought to undermine confidence in the Canadian insurer and its CEO, Prem Watsa.

  • Spyro Contogouris participated in a campaign involving hostile communications, allegations of financial wrongdoing, and efforts to influence journalists and investigators.

  • Short sellers could profit from falling share prices while using negative publicity and financial research to intensify pressure on targeted companies.

  • Taibbi examines how wealthy financial actors could mobilize sophisticated campaigns against opponents without attracting the kind of policing applied to street-level misconduct.

7. Welfare surveillance and unequal fraud enforcement

  • In California, welfare recipient Maria Espinosa experienced an intrusive home inspection after applying for public assistance.

  • Welfare fraud investigations could involve unannounced visits, searches of personal belongings, and scrutiny of household relationships.

  • Riverside County's welfare fraud enforcement program pursued suspected misrepresentations by benefit applicants, including relatively small financial discrepancies.

  • Taibbi contrasts aggressive investigations of poor recipients with large corporate fraud settlements that frequently imposed no individual criminal penalties.

8. JPMorgan Chase and consumer debt

  • After acquiring Washington Mutual in 2008, JPMorgan Chase pursued collections on large portfolios of consumer debt.

  • Former Chase employee Linda Almonte alleged that the bank relied on inaccurate records, defective affidavits, and improper debt-collection practices. She lost her job after raising concerns.

  • Chase obtained judgments against thousands of credit-card borrowers through a legal process in which many defendants lacked the resources to challenge documentation.

  • Taibbi contrasts the severe consequences imposed on indebted consumers with the limited personal accountability of executives overseeing defective collection systems.

9. Expanding enforcement against ordinary citizens

  • In 2011, musician Patrick Jewell was arrested in Brooklyn following a police encounter over a cigarette. His case was eventually adjourned in contemplation of dismissal.

  • Taibbi contrasts routine street arrests with financial regulators' treatment of influential Wall Street figures, including the Securities and Exchange Commission investigation involving John Mack and investigator Gary Aguirre.

  • Aguirre alleged that senior SEC officials obstructed his investigation of suspected insider trading involving powerful financial executives.

  • The book identifies a recurring institutional pattern: ordinary citizens face immediate coercive enforcement, while wealthy suspects often benefit from negotiation, political influence, and regulatory discretion.
