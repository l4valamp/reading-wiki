The Hundred Years' War on Palestine

Rashid Khalidi (2020)

Central argument

The Israeli–Palestinian conflict is a century-long struggle shaped by settler colonialism and foreign intervention, rather than a symmetrical conflict between two national movements. British imperial policy, followed by American military and diplomatic support, enabled Zionist state-building, Palestinian displacement, and Israeli territorial expansion.

Khalidi identifies six major assaults on Palestinian national rights between 1917 and 2014. He also criticizes Palestinian leadership for factionalism, failed diplomacy, and strategically counterproductive violence.

Mechanisms and evidence

1. British sponsorship of Zionism (1917–1939)

  • In 1899, Yusuf Diya al-Din al-Khalidi warned Theodor Herzl that establishing Jewish sovereignty in Palestine would threaten its existing inhabitants. Herzl responded by emphasizing the economic benefits of Jewish immigration.

  • The 1917 Balfour Declaration endorsed a Jewish national home while recognizing Palestinians only as non-Jewish communities with civil and religious rights, not national rights.

  • Under the British Mandate for Palestine, the Jewish Agency received official recognition and developed political, economic, and military institutions without a Palestinian equivalent.

  • Britain violently suppressed the 1936–1939 Arab Revolt, weakening Palestinian leadership and armed resistance before 1948.

2. Partition and the Nakba (1947–1949)

  • The United Nations Partition Plan for Palestine allocated approximately 55% of Palestine to a proposed Jewish state, although Jews constituted roughly one-third of the population.

  • During the Nakba, approximately 700,000–750,000 Palestinians fled or were expelled. Zionist and Israeli forces depopulated hundreds of villages, including through forced expulsions at Lydda and Ramle.

  • Israel prevented most refugees from returning. Property confiscation, including under the 1950 Absentees' Property Law, consolidated wartime displacement into permanent dispossession.

3. Occupation and settlement expansion (1967–1979)

  • The Six-Day War brought the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and Gaza under Israeli occupation. UN Security Council Resolution 242 addressed Palestinians primarily as refugees rather than a people entitled to sovereignty.

  • Israeli governments established settlements, roads, and security infrastructure in occupied territory. The Allon Plan proposed retaining strategically important areas, particularly the Jordan Valley.

  • The Camp David Accords secured peace between Egypt and Israel without Palestinian sovereignty. Proposed Palestinian autonomy left fundamental territorial and political questions unresolved.

4. Destruction of the PLO in Lebanon (1982)

  • Ariel Sharon directed Israel's invasion of Lebanon, seeking to eliminate the Palestine Liberation Organization's military and political presence and establish a friendly Lebanese government.

  • Israel besieged Beirut, forcing the PLO leadership into exile in Tunis.

  • During the Sabra and Shatila Massacre, Israeli-allied Lebanese militias killed Palestinian and Lebanese civilians. Israel controlled the surrounding area; the Kahan Commission found Sharon personally responsible for failing to prevent the massacre.

5. The First Intifada and Oslo (1987–1995)

  • The First Intifada mobilized Palestinians through strikes, boycotts, demonstrations, and local committees, shifting resistance toward popular participation.

  • The Oslo Accords established the Palestinian Authority but postponed sovereignty, borders, Jerusalem, refugees, and settlements.

  • Under Oslo II, the West Bank was divided into Areas A, B, and C. Palestinian authorities administered limited areas while Israel retained extensive territorial and security control.

  • Khalidi argues that Oslo effectively subcontracted aspects of occupation to Palestinian institutions without granting them sovereignty. Settlement expansion continued throughout negotiations.

6. The Second Intifada and Gaza (2000–2014)

  • The Second Intifada followed the failure of the 2000 Camp David negotiations. Khalidi criticizes Palestinian suicide bombings for killing civilians, undermining international support, and strengthening Israeli military retaliation.

  • Hamas won the 2006 Palestinian legislative election. Western pressure and violent rivalry with Fatah contributed to the 2007 division between Hamas-controlled Gaza and the Fatah-dominated West Bank.

  • Israel's Blockade of the Gaza Strip, alongside Egyptian border restrictions, severely constrained movement, trade, and reconstruction.

  • Israeli military operations in 2008–2009, 2012, and 2014 caused extensive civilian casualties and infrastructure destruction. Khalidi connects these operations to the Dahiya Doctrine of overwhelming military force.

7. American diplomatic protection

  • The United States replaced Britain as Israel's principal external sponsor through military assistance, diplomatic protection, and mediation.

  • Khalidi argues that American negotiations repeatedly prioritized Israeli security while postponing Palestinian sovereignty.

  • The Obama Administration opposed settlement expansion rhetorically but did not impose meaningful consequences. Khalidi contrasts this with Obama's willingness to confront political opposition over the Iran nuclear agreement.

Conclusion

Khalidi argues that Palestinian dispossession has been maintained through military force, unequal diplomatic recognition, settlement policy, and international sponsorship. Palestinian political fragmentation and failed strategies have compounded these disadvantages.

He advocates unified Palestinian representation, international solidarity, engagement with Israeli society, and a political settlement based on equal rights rather than indefinite occupation or limited autonomy.
