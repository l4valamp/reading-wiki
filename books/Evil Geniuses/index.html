Evil Geniuses

Kurt Andersen (2020)

Central argument

Beginning in the 1970s, wealthy business interests, conservative intellectuals, and political organizations coordinated a decades-long effort to reverse the economic policies established under the New Deal. Through think tanks, lobbying, legal networks, and electoral politics, they weakened labor unions, reduced taxes on the wealthy, deregulated industries, and expanded corporate power.

The Reagan Revolution institutionalized these changes, while subsequent Democratic administrations preserved many of them. Andersen argues that rising inequality and economic insecurity were not inevitable consequences of globalization or technology, but substantially resulted from political decisions.

Mechanisms and evidence

1. Organizing the conservative counterrevolution

  • In 1971, corporate lawyer Lewis Powell wrote the Powell Memorandum for the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, urging businesses to organize against regulation and criticism of capitalism through universities, media, courts, and political advocacy.

  • The Heritage Foundation (1973), American Enterprise Institute, and Cato Institute developed free-market policy proposals and cultivated political influence.

  • Milton Friedman and Friedrich Hayek supplied intellectual arguments against government intervention. Economic stagnation and inflation during the 1970s increased their influence.

2. Reagan's economic transformation

  • Ronald Reagan's Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 reduced the highest marginal federal income-tax rate from 70% to 50%. Further legislation reduced it to 28% by 1988.

  • During the 1981 PATCO Strike, Reagan fired more than 11,000 striking air traffic controllers, signaling reduced federal support for organized labor.

  • Reagan appointed business-friendly regulators and pursued weaker enforcement of environmental, labor, and economic regulations.

  • Supply-Side Economics promised that tax reductions would stimulate investment and growth. Andersen argues that the resulting benefits disproportionately accrued to high-income households.

3. Reshaping courts and competition policy

  • The Federalist Society, founded in 1982, developed conservative legal networks that influenced judicial appointments and constitutional interpretation.

  • Robert Bork and the Law and Economics movement promoted antitrust standards emphasizing consumer prices and efficiency rather than corporate concentration itself.

  • This narrower interpretation of Antitrust Law helped make corporate mergers and consolidation more acceptable, even when they increased the economic power of large firms.

4. Financialization and shareholder primacy

  • Milton Friedman's 1970 argument that corporations should prioritize shareholder returns became increasingly influential in corporate management.

  • Stock-based executive compensation rewarded managers for increasing share prices, encouraging restructuring, layoffs, and financial engineering.

  • Leveraged Buyouts allowed investors to purchase companies using borrowed money, often leaving acquired businesses responsible for substantial debt.

  • In 1982, the Securities and Exchange Commission adopted Rule 10b-18, providing a safe harbor for certain corporate stock buybacks. Repurchases subsequently became an increasingly important use of corporate funds.

5. Weakening workers and transferring risk

  • Globalization and automation weakened manufacturing employment, but Andersen argues that American labor policies amplified their effects.

  • Declining Labor Unions reduced workers' bargaining power, contributing to a growing separation between productivity gains and wage growth.

  • The shift from defined-benefit pensions toward 401(k) retirement plans transferred investment and retirement risks from employers to employees.

  • Employment-dependent health insurance and weaker job security increased the personal consequences of unemployment.

6. Bipartisan acceptance of neoliberal economics

  • Jimmy Carter supported deregulation before Reagan, including the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978, demonstrating that market-oriented reforms crossed party lines.

  • The Democratic Leadership Council encouraged Democrats to embrace market competition, fiscal restraint, and business-friendly policies.

  • Bill Clinton supported NAFTA, implemented in 1994, and signed the Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act (1999), removing important barriers between commercial banking, investment banking, and insurance.

  • Andersen argues that Democratic acceptance of these policies helped make the conservative economic transformation durable.

7. Corporate influence and political obstruction

  • Conservative organizations and corporate donors financed policy research, lobbying, legal advocacy, and political campaigns to defend favorable economic arrangements.

  • Fossil-fuel interests supported campaigns opposing climate regulation, including efforts to undermine public acceptance of climate science.

  • Citizens United v. FEC (2010) expanded protections for independent political expenditures by corporations and other organizations.

  • Andersen argues that concentrated economic wealth increasingly translated into political influence, making reforms threatening established business interests harder to enact.

8. Inequality and the failure of trickle-down promises

  • After 1980, income and wealth gains became increasingly concentrated among high earners, executives, and owners of financial assets.

  • Andersen argues that reductions in top tax rates, weaker unions, financialization, and shareholder-centered management contributed to this distribution.

  • Other wealthy capitalist countries maintained stronger labor protections and social insurance despite experiencing similar technological and global economic pressures.

  • The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 reduced the corporate income-tax rate from 35% to 21%, continuing a central objective of the conservative economic movement under Donald Trump.
