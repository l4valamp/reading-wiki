The Chickenshit Club

Jesse Eisinger (2017)

Central argument

The Department of Justice gradually abandoned aggressive prosecution of corporate executives, particularly after the 2008 Financial Crisis. Following successful prosecutions in the Enron scandal, corporate lobbying, unfavorable court decisions, and increasingly cautious DOJ leadership weakened the government's willingness and ability to bring complex financial fraud cases to trial.

Instead of prosecuting individuals, the government increasingly negotiated settlements with corporations, collecting large fines without establishing individual criminal responsibility. Eisinger argues that prosecutors became more concerned with avoiding courtroom losses than testing potentially criminal conduct before juries.

Mechanisms and evidence

1. Enron and the decline of aggressive prosecution

  • The Enron Task Force, led initially by Leslie Caldwell, prosecuted executives following Enron's 2001 collapse. Prosecutors Kathy Ruemmler and Sean Berkowitz helped build cases against Kenneth Lay and Jeffrey Skilling, demonstrating that complex executive fraud could be prosecuted.

  • Accounting firm Arthur Andersen was convicted of obstruction of justice in 2002 over document destruction connected to Enron. Its collapse, followed by the Supreme Court's 2005 reversal of the conviction, became a warning against indicting major corporations.

  • Corporate defense attorneys used the Andersen case to argue that prosecution could destroy businesses and harm innocent employees. The DOJ increasingly favored negotiated settlements.

2. The origins of corporate settlements

  • In the 1970s, Stanley Sporkin at the Securities and Exchange Commission investigated corporate bribery and secret slush funds. These investigations helped produce the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977.

  • In 1994, US attorney Mary Jo White's office developed an early Deferred Prosecution Agreement (DPA), allowing a company to avoid prosecution by accepting conditions.

  • In 2003, PNC Financial Services received a DPA after using sham transactions arranged by AIG Financial Products to move troubled assets off its balance sheet. AIG Financial Products reached its own agreement in 2004, paying over $126 million.

  • DPAs became a standard alternative to corporate indictments, allowing prosecutors to announce enforcement victories without proving charges at trial.

3. Corporate resistance to prosecution

  • The 2003 Thompson Memorandum encouraged prosecutors to consider corporate cooperation, including access to privileged information, when deciding whether to indict.

  • In the KPMG tax shelter investigation, prosecutors pressured the firm over its payment of employees' legal fees. Judge Lewis Kaplan ruled that the government had violated defendants' rights, resulting in the dismissal of major charges.

  • Corporate lobbying and defense attorneys pushed back against DOJ demands for attorney-client privilege waivers. The 2006 McNulty Memorandum restricted prosecutors' ability to demand such waivers.

  • The Supreme Court's 2010 ruling in Skilling v. United States narrowed the federal honest-services fraud statute. The Obama administration sought only a limited legislative repair, excluding private-sector corruption.

4. The revolving door and institutional incentives

  • Covington & Burling exemplified the revolving door between corporate defense firms and government. Eric Holder worked at Covington before becoming Obama's attorney general and returned afterward.

  • Lanny Breuer, Obama's criminal division chief, had also worked at Covington, representing major corporate clients.

  • Corporate settlements offered prosecutors fines, publicity, and measurable victories without the expense or risk of trials. Defense firms could negotiate favorable resolutions while charging substantial legal fees.

  • The growing salary gap between government prosecutors and corporate defense lawyers encouraged experienced attorneys to leave public service.

5. Failed prosecutions after the financial crisis

  • After Lehman Brothers collapsed in September 2008, investigators examined its use of Repo 105 transactions to temporarily remove liabilities from its balance sheet. Neither Lehman executives nor auditor Ernst & Young faced criminal charges.

  • The SEC investigated Goldman Sachs's Abacus mortgage deal, in which hedge fund manager John Paulson helped select assets while betting against them. Goldman settled civil charges for $550 million in 2010 without admitting wrongdoing.

  • Former Goldman trader Fabrice Tourre was found civilly liable in 2013. Senior executives were not charged.

  • Prosecutors investigated accounting and valuation practices at AIG Financial Products, including conduct involving Joseph Cassano, but brought no criminal charges against its executives.

6. Prosecutorial caution and abandoned investigations

  • Prosecutor Paul Pelletier spent years investigating AIG-related accounting transactions. His efforts encountered resistance within the DOJ, including under criminal division leadership.

  • Lanny Breuer emphasized the potentially devastating consequences of criminal charges against major financial institutions, reinforcing caution about corporate prosecutions.

  • Investigations involving Countrywide Financial, Washington Mutual, Citigroup, Bank of America, and other financial institutions ended without criminal indictments of senior executives for financial-crisis misconduct.

  • Eisinger argues that prosecutors increasingly treated difficulty proving a case as a reason not to bring it, rather than allowing juries to evaluate contested evidence.

7. Judicial challenges to settlements

  • In 2009, Judge Jed Rakoff challenged a $33 million SEC settlement with Bank of America over disclosures concerning bonuses at Merrill Lynch. He questioned whether the agreement adequately established responsibility.

  • In 2011, Rakoff rejected a $285 million SEC settlement with Citigroup over misleading mortgage securities, criticizing the absence of admitted facts.

  • The Second Circuit overturned Rakoff's rejection in 2014, limiting judicial authority to scrutinize SEC settlements.

  • Rakoff continued criticizing the government's failure to prosecute senior financial executives, disputing DOJ arguments that sophisticated investors made fraud cases too difficult to prove.
